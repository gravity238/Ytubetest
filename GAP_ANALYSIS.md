## 5. Top 3, in detail

### 🥇 Recommended — Sleep

**Working titles (on-pattern, pick one):**
- `What Did Ancient Humans Do in the Middle of the Night?` ← strongest, matches the proven "What Did Ancient Humans Do When…" frame
- `How Did Ancient Humans Sleep?`
- `Why Did Humans Used to Sleep Twice a Night?`

**Why it is unexplored:** the flagship video owns the *day*. Sleep is the other half of the human day and the channel has never touched it — no night video, no sleep video, no dream video.

**The hook (the reframe that makes it travel):** eight hours in one unbroken block is not the ancient human default. It is a roughly 200-year-old industrial artefact. Before gaslight and electric light, much of Europe and early America slept in **two** shifts — a *first sleep*, an hour or two awake in the middle, then a *second sleep*. That middle hour had a name. People called it **the watch**. They prayed, talked, had sex, checked the fire, interpreted dreams, visited neighbours.

**Evidence base (all citable):**
- A. Roger Ekirch (Virginia Tech), *"Sleep We Have Lost: Pre-Industrial Slumber in the British Isles,"* American Historical Review, 2001; and *At Day's Close: Night in Times Past*, 2005. Ekirch assembled 500+ references going back to Homer; later work reports 2,000+.
- Ekirch documents the terms in multiple languages — *premier sommeil* (French), *primo sonno* (Italian) — and shows the phrases appear without explanation, implying readers already knew them.
- Thomas Wehr (NIMH), 1990s photoperiod experiment: subjects restricted to 10 hours of darkness drifted into **biphasic sleep** within weeks, with a waking interval in the middle of the night.
- Classical usage: Thucydides writes "after first sleep."
- Counter-evidence to include for credibility: Niall Boyce re-examined Ekirch's evidence and argued for more variation; Ekirch replied. Modern small-scale-society sleep studies (Hadza, San, Tsimané) do **not** show one universal ancestral schedule — total sleep is roughly 6–7 hours and insomnia is rare. A 10-minute video that handles this nuance is far better than one that repeats the myth "Beautyrest invented 8 hours in 1938" (that claim is unsupported — do not use it).

**Structure for 10 minutes:** modern 3am panic → drop into the cave at full dark → the fire and who fuels it → first sleep → the watch (what they actually did) → second sleep → dawn → the light bulb kills it → Wehr's lab proves the body still wants it → why your 3am wake-up may not be a defect.

**Visual potential:** exceptional. Firelight, total darkness, embers dying, a silhouette at a cave mouth, sleeping bodies in furs, the "watch" as a lit hour. Natural contrast arc from black frames to a cold blue pre-dawn.

**Risk:** low. Nothing here trips advertiser filters.

### 🥈 Teeth

**Working titles:**
- `What Did Ancient Humans Do About Their Teeth?`
- `Why Did Ancient Humans Have Perfect Teeth?`
- `What Did Humans Do Before Toothbrushes?`

**Why it is unexplored:** zero mouth, zero dentistry, zero hygiene across 20 videos. It also sits perfectly against video #11 (sickness) without overlapping it.

**The hook:** for most of human prehistory, cavities were **almost unheard of**. The Mesolithic Korsør Nor man has heavily worn teeth but essentially no decay. Then farming arrives, starch and sugar load up, and the mouth collapses. The horror is that the pain was treated with a **stick**.

**Evidence base:**
- Toothpick grooves in a **1.2-million-year-old** hominin jaw from Atapuerca, Spain — wood fibres preserved in the groove. Previous oldest evidence: a 49,000-year-old Neanderthal.
- Toothpick marks traced back as far as *Homo habilis*, ~1.6–1.9 Mya.
- Cova Foradà, Valencia: Neanderthal teeth, 50,000–150,000 years old, severe periodontal disease with toothpick wear — interpreted as **palliative** dentistry, pain relief.
- National Museum of Denmark: Mesolithic Danes had extreme wear and near-zero caries; children of 5–6 show worn milk teeth; the Korsør Nor man's distinct toothpick mark.
- Neolithic bow-drill dentistry (7000 BC) for treating decayed teeth.

**Visual potential:** outstanding. Extreme close-ups of a stone-age jaw, a twig between molars, a rotten tooth, the first drill. Very frameable.

**Risk:** low; mild disgust is an asset.

### 🥉 Death & burial

**Working titles:**
- `What Did Ancient Humans Do With Their Dead?`
- `Why Did Ancient Humans Bury Their Dead?`
- `What Happens to You If You Die 50,000 Years Ago?`

**Why it is unexplored:** the channel has covered *who else existed* (#3) but never *what happened when one of them died*.

**The hook:** the oldest evidence of grief is not a grave — it is a **healed wound**. Someone survived injuries that should have killed them, for years, because other people kept them alive.

**Evidence base:**
- **Shanidar 1**, Shanidar Cave, Iraqi Kurdistan, excavated by Ralph Solecki 1951–60: crushed left eye socket, right forearm and hand lost, abnormal gait, and advanced auditory exostoses that closed his right ear canal entirely. He lived to roughly 40–50 — decades past the injuries.
- Trinkaus & Villotte, *PLOS ONE* 2017 — the hearing-loss paper. A partly deaf, partly blind, one-armed man would not have survived alone in the Pleistocene. He was fed and protected.
- Important honesty beat: the famous **Shanidar 4 "flower burial"** is now widely doubted — the pollen may have been carried in by burrowing bees. A video that debunks its own most famous legend earns trust.
- Shanidar Z — skull crushed to ~2cm, reconstructed from 200+ fragments.

**Visual potential:** high but somber. Cave, ochre, a grave, a body with a ruined arm, hands placing flowers that may mean nothing.

**Risk:** medium-low. Death content is ad-safe if not graphic; avoid depicting decomposition.
