# What Did Ancient Humans Do in the Middle of the Night?

**POV:** Second person
**Word count:** 1,940
**Projected runtime:** ~10:25 @ 3.1 words/sec (`en-US-AndrewNeural`, `+10%`)
**Note:** channel average runtime is 10:26 — this sits on the money.

---

It's three in the morning, and you are awake.

You're on the sofa. The lamp is on. The television is doing that thing where it's technically off but it's still glowing, and your phone is up at that angle you always hold it, and your face is doing what your face does at three in the morning. Nothing. Just a soft blue wash.

And you're thinking the thing everyone thinks in this chair.

*Why can't I sleep?*

---

This has happened before. Repeatedly, across years, at intervals you've never been able to predict.

And you've probably decided what it means. Your phone. Your cortisol. Your screen time. You've decided you're a bad sleeper, the way some people are bad at maths, and this is your particular defect to carry around.

But for almost the entire history of the species, this was not a defect.

It was a **schedule**.

---

You are not broken.

That hour you spent watching the ceiling do nothing has a name. It is two thousand years old. People said it out loud and everyone in the room knew exactly what they meant.

But something is standing in the way. There's a lie in the road, and we have to break it first.

---

The lie is eight hours.

You've been told your whole life that a normal night is one unbroken block — seven, eight, nine, straight through. One block, no interruptions. That's the target.

That number is not ancient. It is not biological. It is roughly **two hundred years old**.

To understand how a species lost half its night, you have to go somewhere with no lamps.

---

You're in a cave, and it is genuinely, absolutely dark.

Not dark like a bedroom with the curtains shut. Dark like the inside of your own skull. Your eyes have given up trying. There's nothing to see. The dark isn't an absence of light — it's a substance, pressing on your face.

Then, thirty feet away, where you know the fire is, a small orange shape moves.

---

Fire is the entire game.

Every animal in the world has to survive the night, and almost all of them do it by hiding and waiting. Fire is the one thing that lets a soft, slow, entirely edible primate stop hiding.

But fire isn't a thing you have. It's a thing you **do**. It has to be fed — constantly, all night. If nobody feeds it, it dies, the dark comes all the way in, and whatever was standing at the edge of the light gets to come closer.

---

So somebody sits up.

Thin arms, round head, a tan hide over one shoulder, cross-legged on packed earth, feeding a fire that has to stay alive until dawn. They're not being noble. It's their shift.

Sitting up obligates you. Sit with the fire while everyone sleeps, and tomorrow, when there's meat, somebody hands you meat. Keep watch through the dark, and when you're the one who's sick, someone keeps the fire alive for you.

It's a debt system built out of the worst hours of the night. Every single night, you are being measured against your willingness to sit in the dark so someone else can close their eyes.

---

The fire burns down to a bed of coals.

It's barely a colour now. And because the light has pulled back, you can see the mouth of the cave — a rectangle of almost-black, barely lighter than the wall. Beyond it, the world is doing whatever it does when nobody is watching. Wind through grass. Something standing very still out there, listening.

You are inside a ring of light the size of a small room. Everything outside it wants what you're made of.

---

So you lie down.

The furs are warm, which is the point, and they smell like horse and smoke. The rock under you is not flat — your hip finds a stone no matter which way you lie. You can hear the fire. You can hear the person on watch shift their weight. Behind you, someone's breathing has already settled.

You close your eyes. Not because you're done. Because it's dark, and this is what bodies do in the dark.

---

First sleep.

That's the first name. Not a modern word historians invented — the actual phrase, written down in letters and diaries and court records and medical manuals, across hundreds of years, in English, French, Italian. All of it.

**First sleep.**

It came soon after the fire was banked, and it did not last the night. Four hours, maybe. It was often called the *dead sleep*, because it was the heavy one — the drop, the place where you stopped being a person lying down and became a warm weight that was not in the room.

You went down into it and you were gone.

---

Then, around two in the morning, you came up out of it.

Not waking. Not a startle, not a sick feeling. You surface, the way a diver surfaces, slowly, without knowing it's happening. The fire has burned lower. The air has gone colder.

And you are not confused. You know exactly where you are and exactly what hour this is. It's the hour you always wake. It's *the* hour.

You sit up.

---

There's a word for this.

**The watch.** Sometimes *the watching*.

That's what they called the interval. Not insomnia. Not a disorder. Not a symptom. *The watch*, as in the shift you keep, the hours you hold.

And it's not a guess reconstructed by historians. It's written down. People used the phrase the way you'd say *lunch* — something so normal it needed no explanation.

---

**A. Roger Ekirch**, a historian at Virginia Tech, is the person who pulled this out of the archive.

He wasn't looking for it. He was researching the night in pre-industrial Europe and early America — what people did in the dark — and the evidence kept showing up. Court records. Diaries. Prayer books. Medical texts. Poetry.

The same terms, over and over. *First sleep.* *Second sleep.*

French did it too: *premier sommeil*. Italian: *primo sonno*.

By the time he published, Ekirch had more than **five hundred** references, some going back to Homer. Thucydides wrote "after first sleep" the way you'd write "after midnight." *Sleep We Have Lost*, 2001. *At Day's Close*, 2005.

---

So what did you do, in the watch?

Sometimes you prayed. Sometimes you worked — mended, brewed, split wood, wound yarn. Sometimes you checked the animals, or stood in the cold at the door, looking at nothing because a noise had woken something in your chest.

Sometimes you talked, low, in the dark, about things you'd never say at a table with everyone awake. Sometimes neighbours came over. There are records of people walking to the next house in the middle of the night — because the middle of the night was a *known* place, and the people at the next house might well be up.

Sometimes you just lay there and thought about tomorrow, and sometimes you weren't thinking at all, just awake and warm and not required to be anything.

Then you went back to sleep.

---

Second sleep.

And this one was different. Softer. Not the dead drop. People describe the second sleep as the getting-up sleep — lighter, closer to the surface, more where dreams come. You'd wake from it pulled out of something, rather than lifted out of a hole.

You went down again, and it was shallow, and it was long, and then it was light.

---

The cave mouth changes colour.

Not light yet. But the black is becoming not-black — a grey that isn't a grey anywhere else. The fire is out. Somebody's already moving; you hear the specific clatter of a person doing a task with their hands while the rest of the world is still horizontal.

The night is over. You had a first sleep. You had a watch. You had a second sleep. Nobody is going to ask you about any of it, because that's just what a night was.

---

So how did we lose it?

It didn't get banned. It just slowly stopped making sense.

Better lamps came first — gas, oil, better wicks. Then the **light bulb**, and that's the one that ends it. Once light is cheap and instant and independent of the sun, the night stops being a dark gap you have to be inside of, and becomes *time*. Time you could be reading. Working. Paid for.

Bedtime moves backwards. The first sleep swells. The watch gets squeezed down to a sliver, and the second sleep gets squeezed into a nap you're embarrassed about.

By the late nineteenth century it's over. The phrase goes quiet. And anybody still waking at two in the morning, in a place with electric light and eight hours as the target, is now doing something *wrong*.

---

But there's one more piece, and it changes how you feel about that sofa.

In the nineteen nineties, **Thomas Wehr** at the National Institute of Mental Health put volunteers on a light schedule that gave them **ten hours of darkness** a night. No screens, no lamps, nothing.

Within weeks, they split.

They began sleeping in two episodes, with an hour or two awake in the middle, and the waking hour had a specific quality: calm, quiet, without fear and without urgency. They could do that because they'd been given enough time — and enough darkness — to notice they weren't in a hurry.

Your ancestors didn't need a lab. Their bodies did that every night.

And some version of it is still in there. In you. Right now.

---

One honest complication.

Modern studies of small-scale societies — the Hadza in Tanzania, the San in southern Africa, the Tsimané in Bolivia — measured sleep in groups living much closer to an old pattern. They do **not** sleep in two shifts. They sleep one block of about six or seven hours, and insomnia is rare among them.

So they don't show that segmented sleep is the universal default. They show something more interesting: the eight-hour block isn't a law of nature either.

And there's real disagreement. **Niall Boyce** re-examined the historical evidence and argued that Ekirch's sources show segmented sleep *happened*, without proving it was one universal pattern — that there was far more variation than the popular story suggests. Ekirch pushed back and defended it.

The argument is about how widespread it was. Not whether it was real.

---

Go back.

It's three in the morning. The lamp is on. Your phone is up at that angle and the television is still glowing in that way that isn't really off.

You've always been told this is a failure. That you're losing sleep, that you're bad at this, that there's a right way for a body to do the dark.

But there was a version of this where three in the morning was a place with a name, a job, and a reason. Where somebody sat up with the fire because the fire needed a person. Where you woke in the middle of the night and weren't bewildered, because everyone did, and everybody knew what to do with the hour.

You are the only animal that ever stopped hiding from the dark.

You did it with a thing that has to be fed, by a person who has to stay awake, so someone else can close their eyes. The worst hours of the night were the currency, and you paid in them for two hundred thousand years.

The watch is still in you. It's just that nothing is watching anymore.

Put the phone down. It's only the dark.

You've done this for a hundred thousand years. You know what to do.
