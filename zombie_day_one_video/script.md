# Day One: Locked In

You hear the lock turn before you hear the screaming.

The little metal handle dips once. Stops. Then dips again, slowly, as if whoever is outside has all the time in the world.

Your phone lights up in your hand. One alert, pushed through a wall of missed calls: **DO NOT OPEN THE DOOR.**

Something pounds against it.

You stumble backward into the living room. The coffee table scrapes across the floor as you shove it against the door. You drag the couch after it, wedge one end under the handle, and stand there listening to the wood creak.

On the other side, a shoe scuffs the mat.

Then silence.

You tell yourself it’s a neighbor. A prank. Anything but the answer your phone keeps trying to show you. The local news stream is frozen on a reporter’s face. Behind her, people run through an intersection while a traffic light blinks red, red, red.

The stream cuts out.

You’re alone in your apartment, on the first day the world stops making sense.

At 9:17 that morning, it still seemed ordinary. You were on the couch, half watching a video, when the emergency alert made your phone vibrate across the cushion. The message was short and strange: stay indoors, lock all doors, avoid anyone acting confused or aggressive. More information would follow.

Then came a siren. Then another, farther away.

You went to the window. Down on the street, a delivery van sat crooked across the crosswalk. A cyclist was shouting at someone you couldn’t see. People stopped to watch. Someone started filming.

Then the crowd scattered.

You never saw what made them run. You only saw a man sprint into the street, slam both hands against the lobby door, and disappear from view as the elevator chimed behind you.

You locked your apartment door. You checked it twice. You pulled the curtains.

Now it’s 9:26, and the person outside your door is trying the handle again.

You grab your phone and call emergency services. The call connects, but you hear only a breath and a crackle. You say your address. You say someone is outside. A voice begins to answer—then the line drops.

You try again. No signal.

The news stream returns for three seconds. A caption crawls across the bottom: **SHELTER IN PLACE. DO NOT APPROACH THE INFECTED.**

Infected.

That word makes the apartment feel smaller.

You count what you have. Half a bottle of water. A mug of coffee gone cold. A can of soup, two crackers, and a phone battery already below forty percent. You fill every container you can find at the sink. The tap coughs, sputters, then runs clear.

A thud comes from the hallway.

You freeze. Another thud. Not at your door this time—somewhere down the corridor. A neighbor cries out, “Help me!” Then a second voice answers with a low, broken groan.

You back away from the peephole.

The shouting stops.

For an hour, you listen. Footsteps hurry past. A door slams. Someone knocks twice on a nearby apartment, then runs. You stay quiet, because the warning on your phone is the only plan you have.

At 10:41, the power cuts.

The refrigerator falls silent. The Wi-Fi dies. In the dim room, your phone becomes the only light. You switch it to battery saver and check the front door. The couch is still there. The table is still wedged beneath the handle.

Then smoke slips under the apartment door.

You smell it before you see it: sharp, bitter, unmistakable. From the hallway comes a crackle, then a building alarm that starts and stops, starts and stops. You press your sleeve over your mouth and look toward the kitchen window.

The fire escape is outside.

So is the drop.

You open the window a few inches. Cold air pushes in. The metal ladder is folded against the wall, one level below. You could climb down—but the apartment is four floors up, and the window frame gives a dry groan when you lean on it.

A bang hits your front door.

The barricade jumps.

You grab your backpack. Water. Soup. Keys. A flashlight with weak batteries. You leave the rest. You pull the window wider, reach for the fire escape, and stop.

A shape stands on the landing below.

It doesn’t look up. It sways in place, facing the brick wall. One hand drags along it, leaving a slow, uneven scrape.

Your front door bucks again. The couch shifts an inch.

The fire alarm finally holds a steady scream. Smoke thickens behind you. There’s no more waiting for the perfect moment. There is only the next move.

You climb out.

The metal landing rattles under your shoes. You keep low, one hand on the rail, backpack tight against your shoulders. Below, the figure turns toward the sound. You don’t wait to see its face. You climb.

One floor. Then another.

A window opens above you. A voice whispers, “Get inside.” You look up, but the person behind the glass has already vanished. You can’t tell if they’re afraid of you—or what’s coming up the stairs.

The landing below groans.

You climb faster. Your shoe slips on the last rung. The backpack swings; your hand catches the rail. For a second, the street yawns beneath you. Then you pull yourself onto the ground-level canopy and drop into the narrow alley.

The impact knocks the breath from your lungs.

You lie still, listening. The fire alarm wails overhead. Somewhere beyond the alley, a car horn blares without stopping. A siren cuts off mid-note.

You stand.

At the alley mouth, the street is almost empty. A bus sits with its doors open. A paper bag tumbles past your shoes. At the far intersection, people run between abandoned cars—and something follows them, moving in short, jolting bursts.

You grip your backpack strap. The apartment is behind you, full of smoke and a door that won’t hold. The street ahead is full of questions. You have no safe route, no working phone, and no idea who is still human.

But you’re outside now.

The first day has only just begun.
